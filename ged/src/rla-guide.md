## What this test actually is

The GED Reasoning Through Language Arts (RLA) test is **one 150-minute sitting** broken into three timed sections with **one 10-minute break between section 2 and section 3**. It is the only GED subject that mixes reading, editing, and writing into a single score.

**The shape of the sitting**

| Block | How long | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Section 1 | 35 minutes | Reading and language items |
| Section 2 | 45 minutes, hard stop | Extended Response essay (covered in its own chapter) |
| Break | 10 minutes | Leave your seat, drink water |
| Section 3 | 60 minutes | Reading and language items |

GED Testing Service publishes all four blocks as fixed: **35 + 45 + 10 + 60 = 150**. The two item sections are not interchangeable and the clock does not pool. Time you save in section 1 does not carry into section 3, and time you overspend in section 1 cannot be borrowed back. Read the on-screen clock in the first ten seconds of each section and again at its halfway point.

**Question count.** GED Testing Service publishes the RLA as **"about 46 questions"**. Prep sources split on whether the Extended Response is counted inside that 46 or sits outside it; plan for roughly 46 item-clicks across sections 1 and 3 plus the one essay, and do not build a strategy that depends on the exact number. You will also meet a handful of unscored field-test items that look identical to the rest — you cannot spot them, so do not try.

**Item types you will actually see**

- **Multiple choice** — four options, A through D. The bulk of the test.
- **Drop-down** — a word or phrase inside a passage is a clickable menu; you pick the version that is correct. This is the signature GED editing format. The menu almost always contains one right answer and three real-world errors.
- **Drag-and-drop** — move labels, evidence, or events into boxes or a sequence.
- **Select-an-area (hot spot)** — click the sentence or phrase in the passage that does a named job.
- **Fill-in-the-blank** — type a word or short phrase.

**The passages.** Reading sets run **450 to 900 words** with **6 to 8 questions each**. The mix is **about 75% informational** (workplace documents, memos, policy notices, science and history articles, editorials, speeches, how-to material) and **about 25% literary** (fiction excerpts, memoir, occasionally drama). You will see roughly four to six passage sets plus one or two editing passages.

**What a 145 means.** Scores are reported on a **100-200 scale** per subject:

| Band | Score | What it gets you |
|---|---|---|
| Below passing | 100-144 | No credit for the subject; you retake RLA only |
| **GED Passing / High School Equivalency** | **145-164** | Counts toward the credential. 145 on each of the four subjects, never an average |
| GED College Ready | 165-174 | May waive placement testing or developmental courses |
| GED College Ready + Credit | 175-200 | May earn up to 10 college credit hours |

145 is a **performance level**, not 145 points. It means you performed at the level of a graduating high school senior who would pass the subject.

**How raw maps to scaled.** Most items are worth 1 point; a few technology-enhanced items are worth 2. The essay is scored on **3 traits, 0-2 points each**, and that 0-6 result is **double-weighted to 12 raw points**, which GED Testing Service describes as **about 20% of the RLA score** — implying roughly **60 raw points** on the test overall. (GED Testing Service publishes the 12 points and the 20%; the ~60 total is inferred from them, not published.) GED Testing Service does not publish a raw-to-scaled table, because every form is statistically equated so that a 145 on an easy form equals a 145 on a hard one. The working rule that survives every form: **roughly 60-65% of available points lands you at 145.** Concretely — if you get about **30 of 46 items right and score 4 of 6 on the essay**, you are at or just over the line. That is the target. You do not need 40 correct.

**What is supplied on screen.** For RLA: **nothing content-related.** No formula sheet, no dictionary, no thesaurus, no grammar reference, no calculator. Every rule in the cheat sheet below has to be in your head. What you do get are **tools**: a highlighter, a flag-for-review button, adjustable text size and colour, and the passage displayed beside the question the whole time so you can always scroll back. (The on-screen TI-30XS calculator appears on the math, science, and social studies tests - never on this one.)

**Recent changes worth knowing.** The RLA blueprint has been stable — no content overhaul in recent years. What has changed is delivery: the **GED is now routinely available as an online-proctored test taken at home** as well as at a Pearson VUE test centre, and the two versions cover identical content. If you test at home, your workspace must be clear, **no physical scratch paper and no physical erasable whiteboard are permitted at all** (you get an on-screen whiteboard and scratch pad instead), and you cannot leave the camera during the break. Check your state's rules — states set their own age, residency, and eligibility requirements, a few restrict online proctoring, and a small number of states use a different high school equivalency exam instead of or alongside the GED.

## What is on it, by weight

The official content domains are **Reading for Meaning in Texts**, **Identifying and Creating Arguments**, and **Applying Knowledge of English Language Conventions** (the last is commonly labelled "Grammar and Language" and runs about a quarter of the test). The table below breaks those domains into the skills that actually appear as items, with counts out of the ~46 non-essay items. The essay sits outside this table as a separate ~20% of your scaled score.

| Topic | % of test | What they actually ask |
|---|---|---|
| Inference and implied meaning | ~15% (7 items) | "It can be inferred that…", "The author suggests…", "Which conclusion is best supported?" One step past what the text literally says — never two steps |
| Main idea and central idea | ~11% (5 items) | Best title, best summary, "the central idea of paragraph 3", what the passage is mainly about |
| Argument, evidence, fact vs opinion | ~11% (5 items) | Which sentence is the claim; which detail best supports it; which is the weakest support; which statement is an opinion; what assumption the author makes |
| Supporting detail | ~9% (4 items) | "According to the passage…" — pure retrieval, the answer is printed in the text |
| Author's purpose and point of view | ~9% (4 items) | Why the author included paragraph 4; the author's attitude toward X; why a quotation or statistic appears where it does |
| Usage: agreement, tense, parallelism, modifiers | ~9% (4 items) | Subject-verb agreement across an interrupting phrase, pronoun-antecedent match, vague "it/this/they", tense consistency, parallel lists, dangling openers |
| Word meaning in context | ~6.5% (3 items) | "As used in line 12, *reserved* most nearly means…" — including everyday words used in an unfamiliar sense |
| Sentence structure | ~6.5% (3 items) | Run-ons, comma splices, fragments; choosing the correct join between two independent clauses |
| Text structure and organisation | ~6.5% (3 items) | Compare-contrast vs cause-effect vs problem-solution; why the author ordered it this way; the function of a specific paragraph |
| Mechanics: commas, apostrophes, capitals, homophones | ~6.5% (3 items) | its/it's, their/there/they're, possessive placement, comma after an introductory clause, capitalising titles and regions |
| Tone, connotation, voice | ~4% (2 items) | The tone of the final paragraph; why the author chose *cramped* instead of *small* |
| Comparing two texts | ~4% (2 items) | Two passages on one subject: where they agree, where they conflict, how one author would answer the other |
| Transitions and precise word choice | ~2% (1 item) | *However* vs *therefore* vs *furthermore*; replacing a vague or wordy phrase |

The percentage column sums to 100 and the item column sums to 46. **Read the table this way:** reading skills are about **three quarters** of the items (35 of 46) and grammar/editing about **one quarter** (11 of 46). But the grammar quarter is the fastest quarter — those items take 30 seconds each and are pure memorisation. Point per minute, the editing items are the single best return on your remaining study time.

## The cheat sheet

**Nothing here is provided on the test.** RLA gives you no reference sheet of any kind. Every line below has to be memorised. That is the bad news and the good news: the list is finite, and it repeats on every form.

### Sentence structure — the one rule that generates three question types

An **independent clause** has a subject, a verb, and can stand alone. Everything on this test flows from spotting them.

- **Fragment** — no subject, no real verb, or it starts with a subordinator and never finishes. *Because the shipment arrived late.* / *Running through the parking lot in the rain.*
- **Run-on (fused sentence)** — two independent clauses with nothing between them. *The shipment arrived late we missed the deadline.*
- **Comma splice** — two independent clauses joined by only a comma. *The shipment arrived late, we missed the deadline.* **This is the most-tested error on the GED.**

**The five legal ways to join two independent clauses.** Memorise these five; every sentence-structure item is asking which one applies.

1. **Period.** *The shipment arrived late. We missed the deadline.*
2. **Semicolon.** *The shipment arrived late; we missed the deadline.*
3. **Comma + FANBOYS.** *The shipment arrived late, so we missed the deadline.*
4. **Subordinator on one clause.** *Because the shipment arrived late, we missed the deadline.*
5. **Colon or dash**, when the second clause explains the first. *We missed the deadline: the shipment arrived late.*

**FANBOYS** = **f**or, **a**nd, **n**or, **b**ut, **o**r, **y**et, **s**o. These are the only seven words that work with a comma in rule 3.

**Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions.** *However, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, furthermore, instead, otherwise, thus, meanwhile, in fact, for example.* A comma before one of these creates a comma splice. The correct pattern is **semicolon before, comma after**: *The shipment arrived late; however, we still shipped on time.*

**Common subordinators** (put one at the front of a clause and it can no longer stand alone): after, although, as, because, before, even though, if, once, since, though, unless, until, when, whenever, whereas, while.

### Subject-verb agreement

- **Cross out everything between the subject and the verb.** *The box of screws **is** on the bench.* The subject is *box*, not *screws*. Prepositional phrases (*of, in, with, along with, as well as, together with, including*) never change the subject.
- **Always singular:** each, either, neither, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, anyone, anybody, anything, no one, nobody, nothing, one. *Everyone **is** here.*
- **Always plural:** both, few, many, several.
- **Either/or and neither/nor:** the verb matches the **nearer** subject. *Neither the manager nor the clerks **were** available.* *Neither the clerks nor the manager **was** available.*
- **Collective nouns** (team, staff, committee, jury, family, board, audience) take a **singular** verb when acting as one unit — which on the GED is essentially always.
- **There is / there are:** the verb matches whatever comes after it. *There **are** three reasons.*
- **Inverted or delayed subjects:** flip the sentence back. *Behind the counter **stand** two clerks.*

### Pronouns

- **Number must match the antecedent.** *Each employee must submit **his or her** timesheet* (or rewrite as *All employees must submit **their** timesheets*, which GED editing items often prefer because it is cleaner).
- **Vague antecedent is a tested error.** If *it*, *this*, *that*, *they*, or *which* does not point at one obvious noun, the correct drop-down choice is the one that **names the noun**. *The council debated the proposal for hours, which frustrated residents* → *…for hours, a delay that frustrated residents.*
- **Case:** subject pronouns are I, he, she, we, they, who. Object pronouns are me, him, her, us, them, whom. **Test by deleting the other person:** *between you and (I / me)* → *between me*. *Marcus and (I / me) finished* → *I finished*.
- **Who vs whom:** substitute **he/him**. If *he* fits, use **who**; if *him* fits, use **whom**. Both end in *m*.
- **Shifting person is an error.** Do not start with *a worker* and switch to *you*.

### Verb tense

- **Stay in one time frame** across a passage. A drop-down item that offers a past-tense verb inside an all-present passage is testing exactly this.
- **Past perfect (*had* + past participle)** marks the **earlier** of two past events. *By the time inspectors arrived, the crew **had repaired** the line.*
- **Irregular verbs that show up:** lie/lay/lain (to recline), lay/laid/laid (to place), rise/rose/risen, raise/raised/raised, choose/chose/chosen, break/broke/broken, write/wrote/written, see/saw/seen, go/went/gone, do/did/done, bring/brought/brought, drink/drank/drunk, begin/began/begun. **Never** *had went*, *had saw*, *had drank*, *should of*.

### Parallel structure

Items in a series, a pair, or after a correlative must share the same grammatical form.

- *She was responsible for **filing** reports, **answering** calls, and **training** new hires.* Not *…and to train new hires*.
- **Correlatives take matching forms on both sides:** both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, whether…or. *The grant funds **not only** new equipment **but also** staff training* — noun phrase on each side.

### Modifier placement

- **A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence must be immediately followed by the noun it describes.** *Walking to the bus stop, the rain soaked my coat* is wrong — the rain was not walking. Fix: *Walking to the bus stop, **I** got my coat soaked.*
- **Put *only*, *almost*, *nearly*, *just* directly in front of the word they limit.** *She **only** ate the salad* (eating it was the only thing she did to it - she did not cook it or pay for it) vs *She ate **only** the salad* (the salad was the only thing she ate). The GED tests the second pattern: move the limiter next to what it limits.

### Capitalisation

| Capitalise | Do not capitalise |
|---|---|
| Proper nouns: Ohio River, Marcy Alvarez, Tuesday, March | seasons: spring, winter |
| A title **before** a name: Governor Reyes, Dr. Patel | a title **after** a name or alone: Maria Reyes, the governor |
| A region: the South, the Pacific Northwest | a direction: drive south, the east side of town |
| Languages, nationalities, religions: Spanish, Haitian, Buddhist | school subjects: algebra, biology, history |
| Specific course titles: Algebra II, Intro to Nursing | general fields: nursing, accounting |
| *Mom* used as a name: *I asked Mom.* | *mom* after a possessive: *I asked my mom.* |
| First word of a sentence and of a quoted sentence | a fragment quoted mid-sentence |

### Apostrophes and possessives

- **Singular possessive: add 's.** the manager's office, the boss's car, James's truck.
- **Plural ending in s: add only '.** the workers' union, the Johnsons' driveway, three weeks' notice.
- **Irregular plural: add 's.** the children's books, the women's team, the people's choice.
- **Possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe:** its, his, hers, ours, yours, theirs, whose.
- **Apostrophes never make a plural.** *Two manager's* is always wrong.
- **Joint vs separate ownership:** *Ana and Luis's apartment* (shared) vs *Ana's and Luis's cars* (one each).

### Commas — the eight jobs

1. **Before a FANBOYS joining two independent clauses.** *The line went down, and the crew worked all night.* No comma if the second half is not an independent clause: *The line went down and stayed down.*
2. **After an introductory word, phrase, or clause.** *After the meeting ended, we drafted the memo.* *However, costs rose.*
3. **Between items in a series.** *tools, lumber, and paint.*
4. **Between coordinate adjectives** — the test is whether *and* fits between them or you can reverse them. *a long, tedious meeting.* But *three wooden crates* takes no comma.
5. **Around non-essential information** — information you could delete without changing who or what is meant. *Our supervisor, who started in June, approved it.* **Essential clauses take no commas:** *The employee who started in June approved it.* Use *that* for essential (no comma), *which* for non-essential (commas).
6. **Around appositives.** *Maya Chen, the site foreman, signed off.*
7. **Direct address, dates, addresses.** *Thanks, Devon.* *March 4, 2026, was the deadline.* *Toledo, Ohio, has a new plant.*
8. **With quotation tags.** *"We're ready," she said.*

**Where a comma is always wrong:** between a subject and its verb, between a verb and its object, before *that*, before an essential clause, or before a FANBOYS that is not joining two full clauses.

### Homophones — memorise this table cold

| Pair | Rule | Test trick |
|---|---|---|
| **their / there / they're** | possession / place / *they are* | If *they are* fits, it is **they're**. If you could say *here*, it is **there**. Otherwise **their**. |
| **its / it's** | possession / *it is* or *it has* | **It's** always expands. If *it is* does not fit, use **its** with no apostrophe. |
| **your / you're** | possession / *you are* | Expand it. |
| **to / too / two** | direction or infinitive / *also* or *excessively* / the number 2 | **Too** = also or too much. **Two** is countable. |
| **affect / effect** | affect is the **verb** (to influence) / effect is the **noun** (the result) | **A**ffect = **A**ction. *The delay affected morale. The effect was obvious.* Rare exception: *to effect change* = to bring about. |
| **then / than** | **then** = time or sequence / **than** = comparison | **Tha**n goes with **compar**ison; **then** rhymes with **when**. |
| **accept / except** | receive / leave out | |
| **lose / loose** | to misplace / not tight | |
| **whose / who's** | possession / *who is* | |
| **passed / past** | verb / time or position | |

### Transitions — pick by the job they do

| Job | Words |
|---|---|
| Add | also, furthermore, moreover, in addition, likewise |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast, yet, although, whereas |
| Cause / result | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, because, since |
| Example | for example, for instance, specifically, to illustrate |
| Sequence | first, next, then, meanwhile, finally, subsequently |
| Concede | admittedly, granted, of course, to be sure |
| Conclude | in short, overall, in conclusion, ultimately |

### Reading vocabulary you are expected to know

- **Main idea** — what the whole passage argues or reports. **Theme** — the idea about life a literary text implies. **Topic** — the subject in two words; not the same as main idea.
- **Inference** — a conclusion the text supports but does not state. On the GED it is **always one small step**, never a leap.
- **Author's purpose** — to persuade, to inform, to entertain or express. Watch for a fourth: to instruct.
- **Point of view** — in fiction: first person (*I*), third limited (one character's head), third omniscient (all heads). In nonfiction: the author's stance on the issue.
- **Tone** — the author's attitude, carried by word choice. Useful tone words: objective, matter-of-fact, skeptical, critical, admiring, nostalgic, resigned, urgent, cautious, optimistic, indignant, wry, sympathetic, defensive, enthusiastic, wistful.
- **Text structures** — chronological/sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, description, order of importance, claim and support.
- **Evidence types** — statistic, expert testimony, anecdote, example, analogy, historical precedent. Strongest on the GED: **relevant, specific, verifiable data or expert testimony**. Weakest: **a single anecdote or an unsourced claim**.
- **Fallacies that appear in the weak-argument items** — hasty generalisation (one case, sweeping claim), false cause (after this, therefore because of this), either/or (only two options offered), ad hominem (attacks the person), bandwagon (everybody does it), circular reasoning (restates the claim as the reason), straw man (misstates the opponent).
- **Fact vs opinion** — a **fact** can be checked against a record. An **opinion** contains judgement or prediction. Opinion markers: *best, worst, should, ought, unfair, remarkable, too many, the most important*. Careful: *"A 2024 survey found that 62% of workers preferred hybrid schedules"* is a **fact** about an opinion — it is verifiable, so it is a fact.

## How to find the answer

The RLA is not a test of how much you read. It is a test of whether you can locate one sentence. Run the same machine on every item.

### For every reading item, in this order

1. **Read the question stem before the passage.** Four seconds. You now know what you are hunting for, and you read the passage with a purpose instead of a fog.
2. **Read the passage once, at normal speed, start to finish.** Do not skim and do not study. As you go, note only two things: what each paragraph is *doing* (introducing, giving an example, objecting, concluding), and any sentence that states a position. Highlight the position sentences with the on-screen highlighter — you will come back to them for the main-idea and argument items.
3. **Identify the question's type from its verb.** This decides your whole approach:
   - *According to the passage / states / indicates* → **retrieval**. The answer is printed. Go find it.
   - *Infer / suggests / implies / most likely / it can be concluded* → **inference**. The answer is not printed, but one printed sentence forces it.
   - *Mainly about / best summarises / best title / central idea* → **main idea**. Answer must cover the **whole** passage.
   - *In order to / why does the author / the purpose of paragraph X* → **purpose**. Ask what the paragraph does for the argument around it.
   - *As used in line X, ___ most nearly means* → **word in context**.
   - *Tone / attitude toward* → **tone**. Judged by adjectives and verbs, not by subject matter.
   - *Best supports / strongest evidence / weakens* → **argument**. Match the evidence to the exact claim named in the stem.
4. **Go back to the passage and find the line.** Take the most distinctive noun in the question stem — a name, a number, a technical term, a date — and scan for it. The GED does not hide things. If the stem says *paragraph 3*, read paragraph 3 and the sentence on either side of it. If the stem names no location, use the passage's shape: the main idea usually lives in the **last sentence of paragraph 1 or the first sentence of the last paragraph**; counterarguments live in the middle; the author's own position lives at the end.
5. **Answer the question in your own words before you look at the choices.** Say it in six words. This is the single highest-value habit on this test, because the wrong answers are engineered to sound better than the right one, and a prediction immunises you.
6. **Read all four choices. Never stop at a good one.** Choice A is correct roughly a quarter of the time and is often placed as bait when B is better.
7. **Eliminate against the passage, not against your knowledge.** Cross out a choice the moment it commits any of these:
   - **True but not asked.** It appears in the passage and is accurate, but it does not answer this stem. This is the number-one wrong answer on RLA. If you cannot point at the words in the stem that the choice responds to, it is out.
   - **Too big.** It covers the whole passage when the stem asked about one paragraph, or uses *all, never, always, every, proves, none*.
   - **Too small.** One true detail offered as the main idea.
   - **Outside the text.** Correct in real life, not stated or supported here.
   - **Half right.** The first clause matches, the second adds something the passage never says. Read to the end of every choice.
   - **Reverses it.** Says the author opposes what the author actually supports, or swaps cause and effect.
8. **Check by pointing.** Before you click, put your finger (or the cursor) on the exact words in the passage that make your choice true. If you cannot find them, you have an inference item — and then the test is: does one specific sentence force this, in one step? If it takes two steps, it is wrong.

### For every language/editing (drop-down) item

1. **Read the whole sentence, and the sentence before it.** Drop-down items are frequently about the join between two sentences or about a pronoun whose antecedent is in the previous sentence. Judging the highlighted phrase alone is how people miss these.
2. **Diagnose what is being tested** by looking at what changes across the four options. The options tell you the rule:
   - Options differ by **comma vs semicolon vs period vs *and*** → sentence structure. Find the two clauses, ask whether each stands alone, apply the five legal joins.
   - Options differ by **is/are, was/were, has/have** → subject-verb agreement. Cross out the interrupting phrase, find the true subject.
   - Options differ by **its/it's, their/there/they're, your/you're** → homophone. Expand the contraction and see if it makes sense.
   - Options differ by **verb endings (-ed, -s, -ing, had)** → tense consistency. Look at the verbs in the surrounding sentences and match the time frame.
   - Options differ by **he/him, who/whom, they/them** → pronoun case. Substitute he/him.
   - Options differ by a **transition word** → logic. Ask: does the next clause agree with, contrast, or result from the previous one?
   - Options differ by **where a phrase sits** → modifier placement. Ask what the opening phrase describes and check that noun comes next.
   - Options are **the same idea at four lengths** → word choice. The GED wants the **shortest version that keeps the meaning and is grammatically complete**. Redundancy (*past history, free gift, in the event that, due to the fact that*) is the error.
3. **Read your chosen version back into the sentence, whole, in your head.** Hearing it catches what analysis misses.
4. **Default when stuck: the version that is shortest, clearest, and does not stack two independent clauses on a comma.** That default is right more often than chance.

### For the compare-two-passages set

Read Passage A, and in one sentence write what its author wants. Read Passage B, same. Then hold both sentences side by side and ask: same topic, different **claim**? Same claim, different **evidence**? Or different **scope** entirely? Almost every two-passage item is answered from those two sentences plus one supporting line from each.

## The traps

**1. The true-but-not-asked answer.** The most expensive mistake on the whole test. A choice repeats a real sentence from the passage — so it feels verified — but it answers a question nobody asked. **Fix:** after picking, reread the stem word for word and ask "does my choice respond to *these words*?" If the stem says *the author's purpose in paragraph 4*, a choice summarising paragraph 2 is wrong no matter how true it is.

**2. Inferring two steps instead of one.** People talk themselves into elaborate conclusions. GED inferences are small: the text says a worker checked the clock three times, so you infer impatience. It does **not** support "the worker hated the job." **Fix:** ask, "which single sentence forces this?" If you need to chain two assumptions, drop it.

**3. Answering the main-idea item with the most interesting detail.** The vivid statistic or the striking anecdote feels central. It is support. **Fix:** the main idea must cover the **first and last** paragraphs, not just the memorable middle.

**4. Using outside knowledge.** You know something about the topic; a choice matches what you know but not what the passage says. **Fix:** the passage is the only authority. If it says something you believe is wrong, it is still the answer.

**5. Stopping at choice A or B.** Reading three choices instead of four costs several points across a form. **Fix:** force yourself to read to D every time, even when A looks perfect. It takes four seconds.

**6. The comma splice you can hear but do not correct.** Two full sentences joined by a comma *sounds* fine because the pause is natural. It is the most common wrong answer left standing in drop-down items. **Fix:** any time you see a comma in an option, check whether both sides could stand alone. If both can, that comma needs a FANBOYS after it, or it must be a semicolon or period.

**7. *However* with a comma.** *The costs rose, however the project continued* is a comma splice. So is *…, however, the project continued*. **Fix:** semicolon before *however*, comma after. This exact pattern appears on nearly every form.

**8. Its/it's and their/there/they're under time pressure.** These are free points that people throw away by pattern-matching instead of expanding. **Fix:** expand every single time. *It's* → *it is*. If the sentence breaks, use *its*.

**9. Subject-verb agreement across a long interrupter.** *The list of approved contractors and subcontractors (was/were) posted.* The nearest noun is plural, so *were* sounds right. The subject is *list*. **Fix:** physically cross out from the first preposition to the verb.

**10. Confusing tone with subject matter.** A passage about a factory closing is not automatically "angry." Tone lives in the author's adjectives and verbs. **Fix:** find three evaluative words the author chose and let those, alone, name the tone. If the author chose no evaluative words, the tone is **objective** or **matter-of-fact** — and that is frequently the answer on informational passages.

**11. Picking the extreme tone or extreme claim.** Choices containing *outraged, contemptuous, all, never, proves, entirely* are usually too strong for GED passages, which are deliberately measured. **Fix:** prefer the moderate word — *skeptical* over *hostile*, *concerned* over *alarmed* — unless the passage genuinely shouts.

**12. Half-right answers.** The choice opens correctly and then adds a clause the passage never supports. **Fix:** read every choice to its final word before judging it.

**13. Rushing section 1 because it is short, or crawling in section 3 because it is long.** Both wreck the score. **Fix:** the clock discipline in the next section.

**14. Burning the break.** Ten minutes is real recovery. People stay seated, stay tense, and lose accuracy in the 60-minute section. **Fix:** stand, walk, drink water, look at something twenty feet away.

**15. Leaving anything blank.** There is no wrong-answer penalty. A blank is a guaranteed zero; a guess is 25%. **Fix:** see the last-ten-minutes routine below.

## Pacing

**The arithmetic.** 150 minutes total, minus 45 for the essay, minus 10 for the break, leaves **exactly 95 minutes for about 46 items — roughly 2 minutes per item.**

That average hides the real structure, because items are not equal:

| Item type | Target time | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Drop-down / editing | **30-45 seconds** | Pure rule application; no passage hunt |
| Retrieval ("according to the passage") | **60-75 seconds** | One scan, one line |
| Inference, purpose, tone | **90 seconds-2 minutes** | Requires locating and reasoning |
| Main idea, compare-two-passages | **2-2.5 minutes** | Must account for the whole text |
| First read of a new passage | **2-3 minutes** | Paid once, then amortised over 6-8 items |

**The banking strategy.** Editing items are cheap. Every drop-down you finish in 40 seconds banks 80 seconds for a hard inference item later. Do not slow down on grammar to feel careful — you either know the rule or you do not, and staring does not help.

**Section 1 (35 minutes, about 17 items).** Check the clock at the start. At the halfway mark on the clock, you should be halfway through the items. If you are behind, you are over-reading passages — speed up the first read and lean harder on the hunt.

**Section 2 (45 minutes, the essay).** Covered in the essay chapter, but one pacing note that affects this one: **do not let the essay bleed into your mental energy for section 3.** When the essay clock ends, it ends. Let it go.

**Section 3 (60 minutes, about 29 items).** This is where the test is won. You are tired and the section is long. Set two checkpoints: at 30 minutes remaining you should have about 15 items done; at 15 minutes remaining you should have about 22 done.

**When to guess and move on.** Use the **90-second rule**: if you have read the stem, scanned the passage, and you still have three live choices at 90 seconds, you are not going to solve it by staying. **Eliminate what you can, pick from what is left, flag it, move.** The flag costs nothing and the item is now worth 25-50% instead of 0% plus a lost minute. Two items you stubbornly grind out are worth less than the six items you never reached.

**Never leave a screen without an answer selected**, even when you flag it. People plan to come back and run out of clock.

**The last 10 minutes of each item section.** Run this routine, in order:

1. **Fill every blank first.** Scan for unanswered items. Answer each one in under 10 seconds with any choice. This is non-negotiable and takes 60 seconds.
2. **Return to flagged items, cheapest first.** Flagged editing items before flagged inference items — you can often settle a grammar item in 20 seconds with fresh eyes.
3. **Re-check the specific traps.** On any flagged item, ask the two questions that catch most errors: *Did I answer the stem that was actually asked?* and *Is my choice too extreme?*
4. **Do not change an answer without a reason you can say out loud.** "It feels wrong now" is not a reason and changes are net-negative. "I misread the stem — it asked about paragraph 2, not the passage" is a reason. Change it.
5. **With two minutes left, stop reviewing and confirm no blanks remain.** End on that.

## If you only have three days

Ranked by points gained per hour spent. Do them in this order and stop when you run out of time — the top of this list is worth several times the bottom.

**1. The five legal joins between two independent clauses, plus the *however* rule.** (90 minutes)
Run-ons, comma splices, and fragments are the most-tested language content on the test, and the rule set is five lines long. Drill until you can look at any option containing a comma and instantly answer "can both sides stand alone?" Include the semicolon-before/comma-after pattern for *however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless*. **This alone is worth about 3 items and it is the highest-certainty content on the exam.**

**2. The "predict, then eliminate" method on inference and main-idea items.** (2 hours)
Inference is 15% of the test and main idea is 11% — over a quarter of your items, and both are lost to the same two errors: true-but-not-asked, and inferring two steps. Practise by covering the choices, writing your own six-word answer, then uncovering. Do 20 items this way. The discipline transfers to every other reading type.

**3. Homophones and apostrophes.** (45 minutes)
its/it's, their/there/they're, your/you're, to/too/two, affect/effect, then/than, plus the singular-'s / plural-s' / possessive-pronouns-take-no-apostrophe rules. Pure memorisation, instant recall, 30 seconds per item on test day. The cheapest points on the test.

**4. Subject-verb agreement with an interrupting phrase, and the singular indefinite pronouns.** (45 minutes)
Cross out everything between subject and verb. Memorise the *each/either/neither/everyone/everybody/someone/anybody/nobody* list as singular. Two tested items, near-certain to appear.

**5. Author's purpose, point of view, and tone.** (90 minutes)
13% combined. Learn the habit of naming what each paragraph *does* rather than what it says, and of judging tone only from the author's evaluative word choices. Memorise a dozen tone words so you recognise them as choices — and remember that **objective / matter-of-fact is frequently correct** on informational passages.

**6. Argument and evidence, including fact vs opinion.** (60 minutes)
11%. Learn to state the author's claim in one sentence, then judge each choice against *that exact claim*. Memorise that verifiable data and expert testimony are strong, single anecdotes and unsourced assertions are weak, and that a reported survey result is a **fact**, not an opinion.

**7. Word meaning in context.** (30 minutes)
About 7% (3 items). The method is one move: cover the word, read the sentence, say your own word out loud, then find the closest choice. Expect common words in uncommon senses.

**8. Pronoun agreement and vague antecedents, parallel structure, dangling modifiers.** (45 minutes)
About 2 items combined. Worth doing if time remains; the rules are short. For vague antecedents the answer is almost always the version that names the noun.

**9. Timed full-section practice.** (2 hours, do this on day three)
Sit one 60-minute block of about 29 items with a real clock, no pausing. You are not practising content here — you are calibrating the 90-second rule and proving to yourself that you can reach the last item. Most first-attempt failures are pacing failures, not knowledge failures.

**What to skip if you are short on time:** comparing-two-passages technique (4%, and the general reading method covers it), text-structure terminology beyond recognising compare-contrast and cause-effect, capitalisation edge cases beyond titles and regions, and drag-and-drop mechanics — they are rare and the underlying skill is one you are already drilling.

**The target, one more time:** about 30 of 46 items correct plus a 4 of 6 essay puts you over 145. You can miss 16 items and pass.
