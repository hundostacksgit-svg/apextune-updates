## What this test actually is

The GED Science test is a **90-minute, single-block, computer-delivered test of about 34 questions**. There is no scheduled break. The clock starts after the tutorial screens and does not stop.

Say the important thing plainly first: **this is a reading test wearing a lab coat.** Every question hands you a passage, a data table, a graph, a diagram, or a described experiment, and then asks you to read it correctly. GED Testing Service calls the tested skills "Science Practices," and they are built into every single item. You will lose more points to misreading a table than to not knowing biology.

### The hard facts

- **Time:** 90 minutes, one section, no break.
- **Questions:** about 34. The exact count varies by test form, and some items carry more than one raw point, so the raw-point total is larger than the item count. Plan for 34; do not panic if you see 36 or 38.
- **No essay. No short answer.** The two typed constructed-response items that used to sit on the Science test were removed at the start of 2018. Nothing on Science today asks you to write a paragraph. (Extended Response still exists on RLA only.)
- **Calculator: allowed.** The on-screen TI-30XS MultiView is available, and at a test centre you may bring your own handheld TI-30XS MultiView. A **calculator reference sheet** (which buttons do what) is available on screen.
- **There is no science formula sheet.** This is the single most misunderstood fact about this test. The Mathematical Reasoning test has a formula sheet; Science does not. Instead, **any formula you need that is not on the short memorise list below will be printed inside the question itself.** See "The cheat sheet."
- **Delivery:** at a Pearson VUE test centre or online-proctored at home. Same test, same length, same item types. Online requires a green (145+) GED Ready in Science within the last 60 days before your first online attempt in this subject, and is not offered in every state.

### Item types you will actually see

| Type | What it looks like | How it is scored |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice | Four options, A–D | All or nothing |
| Drop-down | A sentence with a menu inside it: "The rate of reaction [menu] as temperature rises." | All or nothing per menu |
| Fill-in-the-blank / numeric entry | A typing box, no choices. You type a number or a word. | Exact match; units usually pre-printed |
| Drag-and-drop | Drag labels into a diagram, a table, a food web, or a sequence | Often multiple points; partial credit possible |
| Hot spot ("select an area") | Click a point on a graph, map, or diagram | All or nothing; one or more clicks |

Multiple choice is the large majority. Drop-down and drag-and-drop are common. Hot spot is rare but appears.

### What the score means

Scores are reported on a **100–200 scale** per subject.

| Scaled score | Band | What it gets you |
|---|---|---|
| 100–144 | Below passing | No credit for this subject. You retake Science only. |
| **145–164** | **GED Passing Score** | Passing. 145 on all four subjects earns the credential. |
| 165–174 | GED College Ready | May waive college placement testing or developmental courses. |
| 175–200 | GED College Ready + Credit | May qualify for up to 10 college credit hours in total at participating colleges. The college, not GED Testing Service, decides what it actually awards. |

**145 is a per-subject floor, not an average.** Scoring 190 on RLA does not rescue a 140 in Science.

### Raw score to scaled score

GED Testing Service does not publish a conversion table, because every test form is equated separately (a slightly harder form needs slightly fewer raw points for 145). What is consistently reported is that **roughly 60–65% of the available raw points lands around 145**.

> Working target: **get 23 of 34 right.** That is 68%, which buys you a cushion above the 60–65% band on any form. Aim for 23; treat 26 as comfortable.

Three consequences of how this is scored:

1. **There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** A blank and a wrong answer are worth exactly the same: zero. Never leave anything blank.
2. **You can miss 11 questions and still pass.** You do not need the hard ones. You need all the easy ones.
3. **Drag-and-drop items with several boxes are worth several points.** Fill every box even if you are guessing on one of them.

### What changed recently

Nothing structural for 2026. The test has been in its current form since the 2014 redesign; the only significant content change since then was the removal of the Science short-answer items in 2018. The item types, the 90 minutes, the 40/40/20 content split, and the 145/165/175 bands are all current.

### The weighting that actually matters

Cross-cut the content weights with the Science Practices and you get the real distribution of *skills*:

| Skill | Roughly how many of the ~34 items |
|---|---|
| Read a table, graph, or diagram and pull out a value or a trend | 10–14 |
| Judge whether a conclusion is supported by the evidence given | 4–6 |
| Experimental design: variables, controls, sample size, flaws | 3–5 |
| Calculate with a formula (given or memorised), including unit conversion | 3–5 |
| Percent, proportion, ratio, probability with scientific data | 3–5 |
| Pure recall of a science fact with no stimulus to lean on | 3–5 |

**Read that last row again.** Only three to five questions on the whole test are pure memory. Everything else is reading.

## The cheat sheet

### The formulas they give you

**There is no science formula sheet on the GED Science test.** What you get instead is this: when a question requires a formula that is not basic, **the formula is printed in the stimulus, directly above or beside the question.** Formulas that routinely arrive this way:

- Specific heat: `Q = m × c × ΔT`, usually with a table of c values
- Kinetic energy: `KE = ½ × m × v²`
- Gravitational potential energy: `GPE = m × g × h`, with g = 9.8 m/s²
- Wave speed: `v = f × λ` (speed = frequency × wavelength)
- Momentum: `p = m × v`
- Pressure: `P = F ÷ A`
- Newton's law of gravitation, half-life formulas, pH scale definitions, concentration formulas, magnification, and anything else unusual

You also get, on screen:

- The **TI-30XS MultiView calculator**, and a **calculator reference sheet** explaining its buttons (fraction key, exponent key, toggle between fraction and decimal, order of operations).

So when you meet a formula you have never seen, **do not panic and do not skip. Scroll up. It is in the box.** Your only job is substitution and arithmetic.

### The ones to memorise

These are the ones the test assumes you already know and will **not** hand you. Learn these eight cold.

| Quantity | Formula | Units | Rearranged |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Density** | D = m ÷ V | g/cm³ or g/mL | m = D × V; V = m ÷ D |
| **Speed** | v = d ÷ t | m/s, km/h | d = v × t; t = d ÷ v |
| **Acceleration** | a = (v_final − v_start) ÷ t | m/s² | |
| **Force** | F = m × a | newtons (N) | m = F ÷ a; a = F ÷ m |
| **Work** | W = F × d | joules (J) | Zero work if nothing moves |
| **Power** | P = W ÷ t | watts (W) | 1 W = 1 J/s |
| **Ohm's law** | V = I × R | volts, amps, ohms | I = V ÷ R; R = V ÷ I |
| **Percent efficiency** | (useful output ÷ total input) × 100 | % | Always ≤ 100% |

Plus three pieces of arithmetic that show up constantly:

- **Percent change** = (new − old) ÷ **old** × 100. Divide by the *old* number. Always.
- **Percent of a total** = part ÷ whole × 100.
- **Proportion:** if the table is a straight line through zero (double x, double y), just scale. 1.5 V gives 0.30 A, so 9.0 V gives 1.80 A.

### Units and conversions

- 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 m = 100 cm; 1 km = 1000 m; 1 L = 1000 mL; **1 mL = 1 cm³**
- 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²; 1 J = 1 N·m; 1 W = 1 J/s
- 1 minute = 60 s; 1 hour = 3600 s
- °C to °F: F = (C × 9/5) + 32. °F to °C: C = (F − 32) × 5/9. Kelvin: K = °C + 273
- Metric prefixes: kilo = 1000×, centi = 1/100, milli = 1/1000, micro = 1/1,000,000, nano = 1/1,000,000,000

### The science-method vocabulary (worth more points than any science fact)

- **Independent variable (IV):** what the experimenter *changes on purpose*. Look for "different amounts of," "at 5 different temperatures," "varied the."
- **Dependent variable (DV):** what they *measure* as a result. Look for "recorded," "measured," "counted," "after 10 days they weighed."
- **Control group:** the group that gets **none** of the treatment — zero fertiliser, plain water, no drug. It is the baseline you compare against.
- **Constants (controlled variables):** everything deliberately kept the same — same soil, same light, same temperature, same age.
- **Hypothesis:** a testable, falsifiable prediction. Often "if... then..." **Not** a guess, and **not** a proven fact.
- **Theory:** a broad, well-tested explanation supported by many lines of evidence (evolution, plate tectonics, atomic theory). A theory does **not** become a law. In science, "theory" is the strong word, not the weak one.
- **Law:** a description of *what* happens, usually mathematical (Newton's laws, conservation of mass). It says what, not why.
- **Correlation:** two things change together. **Causation:** one makes the other happen. Correlation alone never proves causation.
- **Replication:** repeating the experiment. **Sample size:** more subjects means more reliable. **Bias/confound:** any other difference between groups that could explain the result.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from data. **Observation:** what was actually recorded.

### Life science, compressed

- **Cell parts:** nucleus (DNA, control), mitochondria (cellular respiration, ATP), chloroplast (photosynthesis, plants only), ribosome (makes protein, in *all* cells), cell membrane (controls what enters/leaves, all cells), cell wall (rigid, plants/bacteria/fungi only), vacuole (storage, large in plants).
- **Plant cell has** cell wall + chloroplasts + large vacuole. Animal cell does not.
- **Photosynthesis:** 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Happens in chloroplasts. Takes in CO₂, gives off O₂.
- **Cellular respiration:** C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP. Happens in mitochondria, in plants *and* animals, day and night. The exact reverse of photosynthesis.
- **Genetics:** allele = version of a gene. Homozygous = two the same (BB or bb). Heterozygous = one of each (Bb), also called a **carrier** for a recessive condition. **Genotype** = the letters; **phenotype** = what you see.
- **Punnett results to know by heart:**
  - Bb × Bb → 3:1 phenotype (75% dominant, 25% recessive); 1:2:1 genotype (25% BB, 50% Bb, 25% bb)
  - Bb × bb → 1:1 (50% dominant, 50% recessive)
  - BB × bb → 100% Bb, all show dominant
  - BB × anything → 100% show dominant
  - Two unaffected parents with an affected child → **both parents are carriers (Aa × Aa)**, and each later child has a 25% chance
  - Each birth is independent. Three affected children already does **not** change the next child's 25%.
- **DNA:** double helix; A pairs with T, C pairs with G. Gene → protein. Mutation = change in DNA sequence; only mutations in sex cells are inherited.
- **Mitosis:** 2 identical cells, growth/repair. **Meiosis:** 4 cells, half the chromosomes, makes sperm/egg, source of variation.
- **Natural selection needs four things:** (1) variation exists in the population, (2) more offspring are produced than survive, (3) some variants survive and reproduce better in that environment, (4) the trait is heritable, so its frequency rises over generations. **Individuals do not adapt; populations evolve.**
- **Evidence for common ancestry:** fossil record, homologous structures (same bones, different jobs), embryology, and molecular similarity (fewer DNA or amino-acid differences = more recent common ancestor).
- **Energy flow:** producers (autotrophs) → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary → tertiary. Decomposers recycle nutrients. Arrows in a food web point **in the direction the energy flows**, i.e. toward the eater.
- **The 10% rule:** only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next. The rest is lost as heat. This is why pyramids narrow and food chains are short.
- **Symbiosis:** mutualism (+/+), commensalism (+/0), parasitism (+/−). Predation (+/−). Competition (−/−).
- **Carrying capacity:** the population size an environment can sustain. On a graph it is the level where the curve flattens out (S-shaped / logistic curve).
- **Body systems:** respiratory (gas exchange, lungs) + circulatory (transport, heart/blood) deliver O₂ to cells. Digestive breaks food into nutrients; circulatory carries them. Nervous (fast, electrical) + endocrine (slow, hormones) control and coordinate. Excretory removes waste. Immune defends. Skeletal/muscular move.
- **Homeostasis:** keeping internal conditions steady. **Negative feedback** reverses a change (blood glucose rises → insulin released → glucose falls). Sweating, shivering, insulin/glucagon are the standard examples.

### Physical science, compressed

- **Atomic number = number of protons.** It defines the element and never changes.
- In a **neutral** atom, **electrons = protons**.
- **Neutrons = (rounded atomic mass) − atomic number.** Phosphorus: 31 − 15 = 16.
- **Isotopes:** same protons, different neutrons. **Ions:** atom that gained or lost electrons (lost = positive cation, gained = negative anion).
- **Periodic table:** rows = **periods** (energy levels). Columns = **groups/families** (same number of valence electrons, so similar chemical behaviour). Metals on the left and middle, nonmetals upper right, noble gases (group 18) are unreactive. Group 1 alkali metals are the most reactive metals; group 17 halogens are very reactive nonmetals.
- **Trends down a group are smooth** — that is why the test can ask you to predict the next element from a table of five.
- **Bonds:** ionic = metal + nonmetal, electrons transferred. Covalent = nonmetal + nonmetal, electrons shared.
- **Balancing equations:** change **coefficients** (the big numbers in front) only. **Never change subscripts** — that changes the substance. Count each element on both sides.
- **Conservation of mass:** in a closed system, total mass of reactants = total mass of products. If the mass appears to drop in an open container, a **gas escaped**. Mass of gas = mass before − mass after.
- **Physical change:** same substance, different form (melting, boiling, dissolving, crushing). **Chemical change:** new substance formed. Signs: gas bubbles, colour change, precipitate, temperature change, light.
- **Newton's laws:**
  1. **First (inertia):** an object keeps doing what it is doing unless an unbalanced force acts on it. Explains seatbelts, a cloth pulled from under dishes, sliding forward when a car stops.
  2. **Second:** F = ma. More force, more acceleration; more mass, less acceleration for the same force.
  3. **Third:** for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction — **two different objects, equal forces, opposite directions**. Explains rockets, swimming, walking, a gun's recoil.
- **Balanced forces** → no change in motion (still or constant speed). **Unbalanced forces** → acceleration (speed up, slow down, or turn).
- **Work is only done when the object moves in the direction of the force.** Pushing a wall that does not move = **0 joules**, however tired you get.
- **Energy:** kinetic (motion), potential (stored: gravitational, elastic, chemical, nuclear). **Energy is conserved** — it changes form, it is not created or destroyed. Falling object: GPE → KE. Battery: chemical → electrical → light + heat.
- **Efficiency is never 100%** because some energy always becomes waste heat.
- **Heat transfer:** conduction (direct contact, solids/metals), convection (moving fluid, currents, why hot air rises), radiation (electromagnetic waves, no medium needed, how the Sun heats Earth).
- Heat flows **hot → cold**, always. Higher specific heat = harder to heat up and harder to cool down (water's high specific heat moderates coastal climates).
- **Waves:** transverse (up-down motion, wave moves sideways: light, water) vs longitudinal/compressional (motion along the wave: sound). Sound needs a medium; light does not.
- **Amplitude** = height = energy/loudness/brightness. **Wavelength** = crest to crest. **Frequency** = waves per second (Hz) = pitch/colour. **Higher frequency = shorter wavelength = more energy.**
- **EM spectrum, low energy to high:** radio, microwave, infrared, **visible**, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
- **Electricity:** current (I, amps) = flow of charge; voltage (V, volts) = the push; resistance (R, ohms) = the opposition. **V = IR.** **Series** circuit = one path, one bulb out kills the rest, current is the same everywhere. **Parallel** = multiple paths, one bulb out leaves the rest lit, voltage is the same across each branch.

### Earth and space science, compressed

- **Earth's layers:** crust (thin, solid), mantle (thickest, hot flowing rock), outer core (liquid iron/nickel, makes the magnetic field), inner core (solid iron/nickel).
- **Plate tectonics** driven by convection currents in the mantle.
- **Boundaries:**
  - **Divergent** — plates move apart. Mid-ocean ridges, new crust, sea-floor spreading, rift valleys. Rock is youngest at the ridge and gets older symmetrically outward.
  - **Convergent** — plates collide. Ocean + continent = subduction, deep trench, volcanic mountains. Continent + continent = folded mountains (Himalayas). Strong earthquakes.
  - **Transform** — plates slide past each other. Earthquakes, no volcanoes, no new crust (San Andreas).
- **Rock cycle:** igneous (cooled magma/lava) → weathering/erosion → sediment → compaction and cementation → sedimentary → heat and pressure → metamorphic → melting → magma → cooling → igneous. **Any rock can become any other rock.** On a flowchart, name the process on the arrow, not the box.
- **Water cycle:** evaporation (liquid → gas from water bodies), **transpiration** (water vapour out of plant leaves), condensation (gas → liquid, makes clouds), precipitation (rain/snow), runoff (surface flow to rivers/ocean), infiltration/percolation (into groundwater).
- **Weather** = conditions now/this week. **Climate** = the long-term average, 30 years or more.
- **Front passage signature in a data table:** a sharp temperature change, a **wind-direction shift**, and a pressure minimum that then rises. Cold front = sharp temperature drop, pressure rises behind it. Low pressure = clouds and storms; high pressure = clear and calm.
- **Greenhouse effect:** CO₂, methane, water vapour trap outgoing infrared. Burning fossil fuels raises CO₂. Rising global temperature is linked to melting ice, sea-level rise, and shifting weather patterns.
- **Seasons are caused by Earth's 23.5° axial tilt, not distance from the Sun.** Earth is actually farthest from the Sun in early July. Summer = that hemisphere tilted toward the Sun = more direct rays and longer days.
- **Moon phases** come from the changing Sun–Earth–Moon geometry, not Earth's shadow. Lunar eclipse = Earth's shadow on the Moon. Solar eclipse = Moon between Sun and Earth.
- **Solar system order:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, (asteroid belt), Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Inner four are small and rocky; outer four are gas/ice giants. **Farther from the Sun = longer orbital period**, always.
- **The Sun** runs on nuclear fusion (hydrogen → helium). Star life cycle: nebula → main sequence → (small star) red giant → white dwarf; (large star) supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.
- **Renewable:** solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, tidal. **Nonrenewable:** coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear fuel (uranium).
- **Human impact:** deforestation, habitat fragmentation, invasive species, overfishing, acid rain (sulphur/nitrogen oxides), ozone depletion (CFCs), eutrophication (fertiliser runoff → algal bloom → oxygen crash → fish kill), bioaccumulation up food chains.

## How to find the answer

This is a procedure, not advice. Run it on every item.

### Step 1: Read the question stem before you read the stimulus

Not the passage. Not the table title. **The question.** Ten to fifteen seconds. You now know what you are hunting for, and the passage becomes a place to look things up rather than something to learn.

### Step 2: Classify the question into one of five types by its verb

| The stem says | It is a | So you |
|---|---|---|
| "According to the table/graph/passage..." | **Retrieval** | Find the exact row and column. The answer is printed. No thinking. |
| "Which conclusion is best supported by the data?" | **Evaluation** | Test each choice against the data. Kill anything not shown. |
| "Calculate / what is the / how many..." | **Calculation** | Find the formula (given or memorised), substitute, watch units. |
| "Which variable / what is the control / which change would improve..." | **Design** | Find the sentence saying what was changed and what was measured. |
| "Which best explains / what would most likely happen if..." | **Application** | Use one science rule plus the stimulus. Predict before reading choices. |

**Retrieval and Evaluation together are more than half the test.** They need zero outside science.

### Step 3: Work the stimulus in this order

For a **table:**
1. Read the **title**.
2. Read every **column heading and its units** — "Mass (kg)" not "Mass", "temperature change" not "temperature".
3. Find the row/column named in the question. Put your finger (or the cursor) on it.
4. Only now look at the number.

For a **graph:**
1. Read the title.
2. Read the **x-axis label and units**, then the **y-axis label and units**.
3. Read the **legend/key** if there is more than one line.
4. Describe the shape out loud in six words: "rises, then flattens after year 10."
5. Then answer.

For a **passage:**
1. Skim for the **last sentence of the first paragraph** and any **bold/italic term** — the main claim usually lives there.
2. Hunt for the specific noun from the question stem. Read that sentence and the one after it.

For a **diagram:**
1. Read every label.
2. Follow the **arrows** — direction is almost always the point (energy flow, water cycle, rock cycle, circuit current).
3. The question is usually about one arrow or one labelled part. Find it before reading the choices.

### Step 4: Predict before you look

Say the answer to yourself in your own words before reading A–D. Then find the choice that matches your prediction. This single habit kills most distractors, because distractors are built to look attractive *only after* you have read them.

### Step 5: Eliminate with these five filters

1. **Not in the stimulus.** If a choice mentions something the passage/table never gave you, it is wrong on a "supported by the data" question — even if it is true in real life.
2. **Too extreme.** *always, never, all, none, only, proves, eliminates, completely.* Science data almost never supports absolutes. Softer wording (*most likely, tends to, suggests, is associated with*) survives.
3. **Direction flipped.** The choice says "decreased" and the table shows it increased. Check the sign, always.
4. **Right fact, wrong question.** The choice is a true statement from the passage but does not answer what was asked. Reread the stem.
5. **Causation smuggled in.** The data show two things moving together; the choice says one *caused* the other. Unless the stimulus describes a controlled experiment, the safe choice is the one describing the *relationship*, not the cause.

### Step 6: For calculation items, the mechanical routine

1. Write down what you are given, with units: `m = 96 g, V = 12 cm³`
2. Name the formula. If it is not one of your eight, **scroll up — it is printed in the stimulus.**
3. **Convert units first**, before substituting. Minutes to seconds. Grams to kilograms. km to m.
4. Substitute, then compute on the TI-30XS.
5. **Sanity-check the size.** Density of a metal should be a small number like 8, not 1152. Efficiency can never exceed 100%. Speed of a walking person is not 300 m/s.
6. **Check the unit in the answer choices.** If the choices are in kJ and you computed J, divide by 1000.

### Step 7: Check in five seconds

- Does the answer's **unit** match what was asked?
- Did you answer **the question asked**, not the question you expected?
- For numeric entry: is the format right? If the box already shows "g", type `4.6`, not `4.6 g`.
- For drag-and-drop: **is every box filled?**

## The traps

**Trap 1: Answering from what you already know instead of from the stimulus.**
The test writes questions where the real-world true answer is a distractor and the data-supported answer is the key. Fix: on any "according to" or "supported by" item, your hand must physically land on the row, line, or sentence that proves it. If you cannot point at it, it is not the answer.

**Trap 2: Treating correlation as causation.**
Two columns rise together; the choice says one caused the other. Fix: unless the stimulus describes a controlled experiment with a control group, choose the option that says they *increased together* or are *associated*, not the one that says *caused*.

**Trap 3: Flipping the independent and dependent variables.**
Fix: the **independent** variable is what the experimenter *set* (usually the left column of the table, the x-axis, or the thing listed as "5 different amounts of"). The **dependent** variable is what they *measured afterwards* (the right column, the y-axis, the thing recorded on day 10). IV on x, DV on y. Every time.

**Trap 4: Calling the wrong group the control.**
Fix: the control gets **zero** treatment — no fertiliser, plain water, no supplement. It is not "the smallest dose." If every group gets some of the treatment, the experiment has **no** control, and that is often the answer to "what is the main weakness."

**Trap 5: Ignoring units.**
Power in watts requires **seconds**, not minutes. Force in newtons requires **kilograms**, not grams. Density in g/cm³ requires cm³, not mL... which is the same thing, so that one is a freebie. Fix: convert before you substitute, and circle the unit in the question.

**Trap 6: Percent change divided by the wrong number.**
Fix: (new − old) ÷ **old** × 100. The denominator is always the starting value. Dividing by the new value is the single most common percent error on this test.

**Trap 7: Punnett square confusion — genotype ratio vs phenotype ratio, fraction vs percent.**
Bb × Bb gives a **phenotype** ratio of 3:1 (75% / 25%) but a **genotype** ratio of 1:2:1 (25% BB, 50% Bb, 25% bb). Fix: underline whether the question says "show the trait" (phenotype) or "are heterozygous / have genotype Bb" (genotype). Then check whether it wants a fraction, a ratio, or a percent, and convert.

**Trap 8: Reusing 3:1 for every cross.**
Bb × bb is 1:1, not 3:1. Fix: actually draw the 2×2 box. Four seconds. Parent one's alleles across the top, parent two's down the side.

**Trap 9: "Adaptation" described as individual change.**
Distractors say the beetles "turned darker to blend in" or "adapted to survive." Fix: choose the option describing **differential survival and reproduction of variants that already existed.** Individuals do not adapt. Populations evolve.

**Trap 10: Balancing by changing subscripts.**
Fix: only coefficients change. H₂O stays H₂O; you can have 2H₂O but never H₂O₂.

**Trap 11: Believing "mass disappeared."**
Fix: in an open container, escaping gas took the mass. Mass of gas = mass before − mass after. In a sealed container, mass never changes.

**Trap 12: Work done on something that does not move.**
Pushing a parked car with 500 N for 10 minutes = **0 J of work**. Fix: no displacement, no work.

**Trap 13: "Which is most efficient" answered with "which produced the most output."**
A bulb producing 60 J of light from 400 J in (15%) is less efficient than one producing 45 J from 250 J in (18%). Fix: efficiency is always a **ratio**. Compute all four.

**Trap 14: Misreading a trend that changes.**
"The population increased" vs "the population increased then levelled off." Fix: check the last two or three data points separately from the first two or three.

**Trap 15: Seasons caused by distance.**
Fix: **axial tilt**. Earth is farthest from the Sun in July.

**Trap 16: Hypothesis/theory/law mixed up.**
Fix: hypothesis = testable prediction before the experiment; theory = big, well-supported explanation; law = a description of what always happens, usually a formula. A theory never "graduates" into a law.

**Trap 17: Leaving a drag-and-drop box empty, or leaving anything blank.**
Fix: no penalty for wrong answers. Fill every box, answer every item, flag and move.

**Trap 18: Burning six minutes on one hard calculation.**
Fix: two and a half minutes maximum. Guess, flag, move. That one item is worth the same as the easy retrieval item you will now run out of time for.

## Pacing

**90 minutes ÷ 34 questions = 2.6 minutes each.** That sounds generous and it is, but only if you refuse to donate time to hard items.

### The plan

- Target **2 minutes** on a typical item and bank the difference.
- Retrieval items ("according to the table") should take **under 60 seconds.** Take the profit.
- Hard calculation or multi-step evaluation items: **2.5 minutes, hard stop.** Then guess, flag, move.

### Checkpoints — write these on your scratch paper in the first minute

| At this time | You should be finishing question |
|---|---|
| 25 minutes | 10 |
| 45 minutes | 18 |
| 65 minutes | 26 |
| 80 minutes | 34 (all of them) |

If you are behind at any checkpoint, do not speed up on the current question — **change your triage instead**: on the next long passage, read the question, do one targeted look-up, and if nothing jumps out in 45 seconds, guess and flag.

### When to guess and move on

Guess immediately if **any** of these is true:

- You have read the stimulus twice and still cannot find the relevant data.
- You cannot identify which formula applies, and none is printed.
- You have eliminated two choices and are stuck between the last two for more than 30 seconds. **Pick one and move.** Stuck-between-two is a coin flip that does not improve with staring.
- The item involves a long unfamiliar passage and you are behind schedule.

**Guessing rules:** if you must guess blind, prefer the choice with the **softer wording** (*most likely, tends to*) over absolutes, and prefer the choice that stays **inside the stimulus**. Never leave blank — a blank and a wrong answer score the same, but a guess has a 25% chance.

### The last 10 minutes

Do these in this exact order:

1. **Sweep for blanks.** Use the item review screen. Every unanswered item gets an answer, even a blind guess. This is worth more than anything else you could do with the time.
2. **Check every drag-and-drop and hot spot for empty boxes.** Partial credit is real; an empty box is a guaranteed zero.
3. **Check numeric-entry format.** No units typed in if the unit is pre-printed, no commas in a number if the box rejects them, decimals where decimals belong.
4. **Revisit flagged items, hardest last.** Start with flagged items where you had eliminated two choices — those are closest to being won.
5. **Do not re-open items you answered confidently.** Changing a confident answer under time pressure loses more points than it gains.

If you finish early, that is fine and normal. Use the spare minutes on step 1 and step 4 only.

## If you only have three days

Ranked by points-per-hour-of-study. Do them in this order and stop when you run out of time — the top of this list is worth several times the bottom.

**1. Table and graph reading drills (highest yield, by a distance).**
Do 40 "according to the table/graph" questions. Practise the routine: title, column headings with units, legend, find the row, read the number. Force yourself to touch the data before choosing. This is 10–14 questions on your test and requires no science.

**2. "Is this conclusion supported?" drills.**
Learn the elimination filters cold: not in the stimulus, too extreme, direction flipped, causation smuggled in. Practise saying *why* each wrong choice is wrong. Worth 4–6 questions.

**3. Variables, controls, and experimental design.**
Twenty minutes, then practise. IV = what was changed. DV = what was measured. Control = the group that got none. Weaknesses = small sample, no control, groups self-selected, only one trial, a confounding difference. Worth 3–5 questions.

**4. The eight memorised formulas plus unit conversion plus percent change.**
Density, speed, acceleration, F = ma, W = Fd, P = W/t, V = IR, percent efficiency. Then drill: minutes→seconds, grams→kilograms, and (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Worth 3–5 questions, and they are fast points.

**5. Punnett squares.**
Thirty minutes. Draw the 2×2. Memorise Bb × Bb = 3:1 (75/25), Bb × bb = 1:1 (50/50), two unaffected carriers → 25% affected. Know genotype vs phenotype and fraction vs percent. Usually 2–3 questions and they are very gettable.

**6. Photosynthesis, respiration, and the 10% energy rule.**
Both equations, which organelle, what goes in and what comes out, and energy pyramids. Practise "24,000 kcal at producers, how much at the third level" — divide by 10 twice. Worth 2–4 questions.

**7. Newton's three laws and work/energy/power vocabulary.**
Be able to read a scenario and name the law. Know that no movement means no work, and that efficiency is a ratio. Worth 2–4 questions.

**8. Atoms, the periodic table, balancing, and conservation of mass.**
Atomic number = protons = electrons (neutral); neutrons = mass − atomic number; groups behave alike; coefficients only when balancing; mass before = mass after. Worth 3–4 questions.

**9. Earth science core four:** plate boundary types, the rock cycle flowchart, the water cycle terms (especially **transpiration**), and the greenhouse effect. Worth 3–4 questions for about an hour of work.

**10. Natural selection's four conditions, plus hypothesis vs theory vs law.**
Fifteen minutes each. Both show up as one clean question with an obvious wrong-answer trap.

### What to skip if you are short on time

Skip memorising the EM spectrum in detail, star life cycles beyond "fusion powers the Sun," organelle lists beyond the seven above, the nitrogen cycle's detailed steps, and any chemistry past balancing. These are at most one question each, and they are the ones you can afford to miss while still clearing 145.

### The night before

- Reread **The cheat sheet** once, and **The traps** twice.
- Work 10 mixed practice questions, timed at 2 minutes each, to set your internal clock.
- Do not learn anything new. Sleep.
