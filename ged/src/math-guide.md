## What this test actually is

GED Mathematical Reasoning is a single computer-delivered test, **115 minutes**, **46 questions**, worth about **49 raw score points** (a handful of items are worth 2 points, which is why the point total is higher than the item count). There is no essay and nothing is hand-scored. You get your score the same day, usually within a few hours.

It is delivered in two parts inside those 115 minutes:

- **Part 1 — the first 5 questions. No calculator.** No calculator button appears on screen and you may not have a handheld one out. You must submit Part 1 before Part 2 opens, and **you cannot go back to Part 1 afterwards**. Treat these five as their own mini-test.
- **A 3-minute break** between the parts. At a test center this is when you retrieve your handheld calculator if you brought one. The break does not come out of your 115 minutes.
- **Part 2 — the remaining 41 questions. Calculator allowed.** An on-screen **TI-30XS MultiView** is built into the screen. At a physical test center you may instead use your own approved handheld TI-30XS MultiView. For online-proctored testing you must use the on-screen one — no handheld, no scratch paper beyond the erasable board or whiteboard the proctor approves.

The clock runs for all 115 minutes across both parts. You can flag questions and come back to them **within** a part, not across parts.

### Item types you will actually see

- **Multiple choice** — four options, A through D. This is the majority of the test.
- **Fill-in-the-blank / numeric entry** — a box, no choices. You type the number. These are the ones where guessing is worthless and sloppy arithmetic is fatal. Type digits, a decimal point, and a minus sign only; do not type units, commas, dollar signs, or the word "percent" unless the box shows one already.
- **Drop-down** — a sentence with a menu embedded in it: "The slope of the line is [▼] and the y-intercept is [▼]."
- **Drag-and-drop** — you drag numbers, expressions, or labels into slots: building an equation, ordering values, completing a table.
- **Hot spot** — you click a point directly on a coordinate grid or number line. Used for plotting points, marking an intercept, or showing a solution to an inequality.

Items appear either standalone or in a **scenario** — one table, graph, or short setup followed by two or three questions that all use it. When you see a scenario, read the stimulus once properly, because you will be paid for it three times.

### On-screen tools available throughout

- **Formula Sheet** button — the official GED Mathematics Formula Sheet, reproduced in full below. Available on both parts, including the no-calculator part.
- **Calculator Reference Sheet** button — a one-page guide to the TI-30XS keys, available in Part 2.
- **Flag for Review**, a question navigator, and an on-screen note/scratch area. Physical scratch paper or an erasable note board is supplied at the test center.

### What 145 means, and how raw score becomes scaled score

Every GED subject is reported on a **100–200** scale. The bands are identical for all four subjects:

| Scaled score | Band | What it gets you |
|---|---|---|
| 100–144 | Below Passing | No credit for that subject. You must retake it. |
| 145–164 | **GED Passing Score / High School Equivalency** | You pass. This is the only line that matters for the credential. |
| 165–174 | GED College Ready | Signals you can skip developmental/remedial college math placement at many colleges. |
| 175–200 | GED College Ready + Credit | May earn up to a few actual college credits at participating institutions. |

Things that are true and that people get wrong:

- **Scores are not averaged across subjects.** A 190 in Social Studies does not rescue a 140 in Math. Each of the four subjects must independently reach 145.
- **145 is a scaled score, not a percentage.** There is no fixed "you need 30 right." GED uses item-response-theory equating, so an easier form needs slightly more raw points than a harder one. As a planning number, **roughly 60–65% of the raw points** lands near 145 — call it **about 30 of the 49 raw points**, i.e. roughly 29–32 questions right out of 46.
- **There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** A blank and a wrong answer score identically: zero. **Never leave anything blank**, including numeric-entry boxes — put a plausible number in.
- 145 is defined as performance comparable to a graduating high school senior. It is a genuinely passable bar in a week of focused work, because a large share of the test is arithmetic, percent, and straight-line algebra.

### What has changed recently

The 2014-generation test blueprint (115 minutes, 46 items, 45/55 quantitative/algebraic split, TI-30XS, 145 pass line) is still the operating structure. The visible recent changes are: the **Mathematics Formula Sheet was reissued in a revised 2026 edition** (same formulas, cleaner typesetting and wording), the short-answer item type was removed from the test suite years ago and does not appear in math at all, and the item mix has drifted toward **real-world, multi-step problems and data interpretation** rather than bare computation. Nothing about the calculator policy, the timing, or the score bands has moved.

## What is on it, by weight

The official blueprint splits the test into two domains and four reporting areas:

| Topic | % of test | What they actually ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantitative problem solving with rational numbers** | 25% (~11–12 items) | Order of operations; integers with negatives; adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions and decimals in a real scenario; ratios and unit rates; proportions and scale; percent of, percent off, tax, tip, markup, commission, percent increase and decrease, finding the original amount; simple interest; exponents, square and cube roots; scientific notation; absolute value; comparing and ordering numbers |
| **Quantitative problem solving in measurement** | 20% (~9–10 items) | Area, perimeter and circumference including composite L-shapes and part-circles; volume and surface area of prisms, cylinders, cones, pyramids and spheres; the Pythagorean theorem in a real setting (ladder, ramp, diagonal); unit conversion within and between systems; mean, median, mode, range, weighted averages; reading bar graphs, line graphs, dot plots, histograms, box plots, tables; probability of one event and of two events; counting with the multiplication principle |
| **Algebraic problem solving with expressions and equations** | 30% (~13–14 items) | Writing an expression from a sentence; evaluating expressions; combining like terms and distributing; solving one- and two-step and multi-step linear equations including ones with fractions and variables on both sides; rearranging a formula for a named variable; linear inequalities and their meaning in context; systems of two linear equations solved by substitution or elimination, usually from a word problem; adding, subtracting and multiplying polynomials; factoring out a GCF, factoring trinomials, difference of squares; solving quadratics by factoring and by the quadratic formula |
| **Algebraic problem solving with graphs and functions** | 25% (~11–12 items) | Plotting and reading points; slope from two points, from a graph, from a table, and what the slope **means** in the story; y-intercept and what it means; writing the equation of a line in slope-intercept or point-slope form; x- and y-intercepts; parallel and perpendicular slopes; function notation f(x), evaluating and solving f(x) = k; reading a function from a table or graph; comparing two functions given in different forms; domain and range; identifying linear vs. quadratic vs. exponential behaviour; arithmetic and geometric sequences |

Domain totals: **Quantitative problem solving 45%**, **Algebraic problem solving 55%**. If you are triaging, the algebra half is bigger **and** more formulaic — it rewards study faster than the geometry does.

## The cheat sheet

### ON THE SCREEN — the official GED Mathematics Formula Sheet, reproduced

This is what appears when you click **Formula Sheet**. Do not memorise these; memorise **where they are on the sheet** so you can find one in four seconds.

**Area of a:**

- square: **A = s²**
- rectangle: **A = lw**
- parallelogram: **A = bh**
- triangle: **A = ½bh**
- trapezoid: **A = ½h(b₁ + b₂)**
- circle: **A = πr²**

**Perimeter of a:**

- square: **P = 4s**
- rectangle: **P = 2l + 2w**
- triangle: **P = s₁ + s₂ + s₃**
- Circumference of a circle: **C = 2πr** OR **C = πd**; **π ≈ 3.14**

**Surface Area and Volume of a:**

- rectangular/right prism: **SA = ph + 2B**, **V = Bh**
- cylinder: **SA = 2πrh + 2πr²**, **V = πr²h**
- pyramid: **SA = ½ps + B**, **V = ⅓Bh**
- cone: **SA = πrs + πr²**, **V = ⅓πr²h**
- sphere: **SA = 4πr²**, **V = (4/3)πr³**
- (**p** = perimeter of base, **B** = area of base, **π ≈ 3.14**)

**Data**

- **mean** = the sum of the values of a data set divided by the number of values in the data set
- **median** = the middle value in an odd number of ordered values, or the mean of the two middle values in an even number of ordered values

**Algebra**

- slope of a line: **m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)**
- slope-intercept form of the equation of a line: **y = mx + b**
- point-slope form of the equation of a line: **y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)**
- standard form of a quadratic equation: **y = ax² + bx + c**
- quadratic formula: **x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / (2a)**
- Pythagorean theorem: **a² + b² = c²**
- simple interest: **I = prt** (I = interest, p = principal, r = rate, t = time)
- distance formula: **d = rt**
- total cost: **total cost = (number of units) × (price per unit)**

> A correction worth having in your head: a great many prep sites tell you the quadratic formula, simple interest, d = rt and the slope formula are **not** given. They are. All four are on the sheet, in the Algebra block, and have been for years. Do not waste a day of a three-day plan memorising things the screen hands you.

### NOT on the sheet — memorise these

Everything below is tested and nothing below is supplied. This is the real memorisation list.

**Percent**

- part = percent × whole, so **percent = part ÷ whole** and **whole = part ÷ percent**
- **percent change = (new − old) ÷ old**, then ×100. Positive is an increase, negative a decrease. **The denominator is always the ORIGINAL.**
- percent increase shortcut: new = old × (1 + r). A 15% increase is **× 1.15**.
- percent decrease shortcut: new = old × (1 − r). A 30% discount is **× 0.70**.
- reverse percent: if 117 is 65% of something, the something is **117 ÷ 0.65**.
- successive percents multiply, they do not add: up 20% then down 20% is × 1.20 × 0.80 = **× 0.96**, a 4% net loss.

**Coordinate geometry**

- **midpoint = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)**
- **distance between two points = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)** — this is the Pythagorean theorem in disguise; the sheet's "distance formula" is d = rt, which is a different thing entirely
- **parallel lines have equal slopes**; **perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals** (2 and −½, −3/4 and 4/3)
- horizontal line: slope 0, equation **y = k**. Vertical line: slope **undefined**, equation **x = k**.
- x-intercept: set **y = 0**. y-intercept: set **x = 0**.
- vertex of y = ax² + bx + c is at **x = −b/(2a)**; a > 0 opens up (minimum), a < 0 opens down (maximum)

**Exponent rules**

- **xᵃ · xᵇ = x⁽ᵃ⁺ᵇ⁾**
- **xᵃ ÷ xᵇ = x⁽ᵃ⁻ᵇ⁾**
- **(xᵃ)ᵇ = x⁽ᵃᵇ⁾**
- **(xy)ᵃ = xᵃyᵃ**
- **x⁰ = 1** for any x ≠ 0
- **x⁻ᵃ = 1/xᵃ**
- **x^(1/2) = √x**, **x^(1/3) = ∛x**, **x^(a/b) = ᵇ√(xᵃ)**
- scientific notation: a × 10ⁿ with **1 ≤ a < 10**. Multiplying? Multiply the fronts, add the exponents. Dividing? Divide the fronts, subtract the exponents.

**Special products and factoring**

- **(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²**
- **(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²**
- **(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²** (difference of squares)
- **x² + bx + c** factors as (x + m)(x + n) where **m × n = c** and **m + n = b**
- always pull out the **GCF first**
- FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last

**Statistics beyond mean and median**

- **mode** = the value that appears most often (there can be none, one, or several)
- **range** = largest − smallest
- **weighted mean** = Σ(value × weight) ÷ Σ(weights)
- to find the score you need for a target mean: **needed = target × (number of items) − (current total)**
- the mean is dragged by outliers; the median is not. If a question says "which better represents a typical value when one value is extreme," the answer is the **median**.

**Probability and counting**

- **P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes**, always between 0 and 1
- **P(not A) = 1 − P(A)**
- independent events, "and": **multiply**
- mutually exclusive events, "or": **add**
- "without replacement" means the second denominator drops by 1
- fundamental counting principle: choices multiply — 4 shirts × 3 pants = 12 outfits

**Sequences**

- arithmetic (add the same each time): **aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d**
- geometric (multiply by the same each time): **aₙ = a₁ · r⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾**
- exponential growth/decay: **A = P(1 + r)ᵗ** for growth, **A = P(1 − r)ᵗ** for decay

**Unit conversions that are not always supplied**

- 12 in = 1 ft; 3 ft = 1 yd; 5,280 ft = 1 mi
- 16 oz = 1 lb; 2,000 lb = 1 ton
- 8 fl oz = 1 cup; 2 cups = 1 pint; 2 pints = 1 quart; 4 quarts = 1 gallon
- 60 sec = 1 min; 60 min = 1 hr; 24 hr = 1 day
- metric: kilo = 1,000; centi = 1/100; milli = 1/1,000. Move the decimal.
- 1 in ≈ 2.54 cm; 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb; 1 mi ≈ 1.6 km (the test usually gives you any cross-system rate it wants used — use the one on the screen, not the one in your memory)

**Number facts to have cold**

- squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225 (that is 1² through 15²)
- cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216
- fraction/decimal/percent: ½ = 0.5 = 50%; ⅓ ≈ 0.333 = 33⅓%; ¼ = 0.25 = 25%; ⅕ = 0.2 = 20%; ⅛ = 0.125 = 12.5%; ⅔ ≈ 0.667 = 66⅔%; ¾ = 0.75 = 75%; ⅜ = 0.375 = 37.5%; ⅝ = 0.625 = 62.5%
- PEMDAS: Parentheses, Exponents, then Multiply/Divide **left to right**, then Add/Subtract **left to right**

### The calculator

The on-screen calculator is a **TI-30XS MultiView**, opened by a Calculator button in Part 2 only. There is also a **Calculator Reference Sheet** button beside it. If you test at a center you may bring your own approved handheld TI-30XS MultiView, which is faster to press; online-proctored test takers must use the on-screen one and should practise clicking it.

The single most valuable thing you can do the night before: open the free GED practice test and press every key below once, so none of it is new.

**The two minus keys — this loses more points than any other calculator issue**

- **`(-)`** is the **negative sign**. It makes a number negative. It sits near the bottom of the keypad next to the decimal point.
- **`−`** is **subtraction**. It sits in the right-hand column with `+`, `×`, `÷`.
- To compute −8 + 3: press `(-)` `8` `+` `3` `enter` → **−5**.
- To compute 5 − 12: press `5` `−` `1` `2` `enter` → **−7**.
- To compute (−4)²: press `(` `(-)` `4` `)` `x²` `enter` → **16**. Without the parentheses, `(-)` `4` `x²` gives **−16**, because the calculator squares 4 and then negates. This exact miskey is a classic GED wrong answer.

**Fractions**

- The fraction key is printed **`n/d`**, with **`Un/d`** as its 2nd function.
- To enter 3/4: press `n/d`, type `3`, press `▼` (down arrow) to drop into the denominator, type `4`, then press `►` (right arrow) to step **out** of the fraction before you type anything else. Forgetting the `►` is why people end up with 3/(4+2) instead of 3/4 + 2.
- Full example, 3/4 + 2/5: `n/d` `3` `▼` `4` `►` `+` `n/d` `2` `▼` `5` `►` `enter` → **23/20**.

**Mixed numbers**

- Type the whole number, then press `2nd` `n/d` (that is `Un/d`), then the numerator, `▼`, the denominator, `►`.
- Example, 2¾ + 1½: `2` `2nd` `n/d` `3` `▼` `4` `►` `+` `1` `2nd` `n/d` `1` `▼` `2` `►` `enter` → **4¼**.
- **Bulletproof fallback if the template fights you:** convert to an improper fraction yourself and use the plain `n/d` key. 2¾ = 11/4, 1½ = 3/2. `n/d` `11` `▼` `4` `►` `+` `n/d` `3` `▼` `2` `►` `enter` gives **17/4**, which is the same 4¼. Numeric-entry boxes accept either form unless the box is a mixed-number template.
- To flip an improper fraction answer to a mixed number, press `2nd` then the toggle described next.

**The fraction ↔ decimal toggle**

- The key printed with two arrows, **`◄►`**, toggles the **answer** between fraction and decimal, between exact √ and decimal, and between exact π and decimal. Press it after `enter`.
- Example: `n/d` `5` `▼` `8` `►` `enter` shows **5/8**; press `◄►` and it shows **0.625**.
- **Bulletproof fallback:** to force a decimal, just divide (`5` `÷` `8` `enter` → 0.625), or put a decimal point in any number in the expression. To go decimal → fraction, the toggle is the only quick route, so practise it.
- Numeric-entry boxes: if the question says "round to the nearest hundredth" you must type the decimal; if it says "express as a fraction" type the fraction. Toggle to whichever the question asked for before you copy it across.

**Exponents and roots**

- **`x²`** squares whatever is on screen: `1` `4` `x²` `enter` → **196**.
- **`^`** raises to any power: `2` `^` `5` `enter` → **32**. Use `►` to step out of the exponent before continuing.
- **`2nd` `x²`** is **`√`** (square root): `2nd` `x²` `2` `2` `5` `►` `enter` → **15**.
- **`2nd` `^`** is the **nth root**, and the index goes **first**: `3` `2nd` `^` `2` `7` `►` `enter` → **3** (the cube root of 27).
- Negative exponents: `2` `^` `(-)` `3` `enter` → **1/8** (toggle for 0.125). Note the `(-)`, not `−`.

**Scientific notation**

- The key is printed **`x10ⁿ`**. Do not type "× 10 ^".
- 3.6 × 10⁵: `3` `.` `6` `x10ⁿ` `5` `enter` → **360000**.
- 4.2 × 10⁻³: `4` `.` `2` `x10ⁿ` `(-)` `3` `enter` → **0.0042**.
- (2 × 10⁴)(3 × 10⁵): `2` `x10ⁿ` `4` `×` `3` `x10ⁿ` `5` `enter` → **6 × 10⁹**, displayed as 6E9 or 6×10⁹ depending on the mode.

**Percent**

- `%` is a 2nd function — press `2nd` then the key showing `%` above it, right after typing the number. `1` `5` `2nd %` turns 15 into 0.15 on screen.
- **Bulletproof fallback, and honestly the faster habit: just type the decimal.** 15% is `0.15`. 8.25% is `0.0825`. 140% is `1.4`. You will never misfire a 2nd-function key that you are not pressing.
- 18% tip on a 46.50 dollar bill: `4` `6` `.` `5` `0` `×` `0` `.` `1` `8` `enter` → **8.37**.
- Price after a 30% discount on 85 dollars: `8` `5` `×` `0` `.` `7` `enter` → **59.50**. One multiplication, not two steps.

**π**

- There is a dedicated **`π`** key. Use it rather than typing 3.14, then round at the very end. If the question or the answer choices clearly used 3.14, and your π answer is off by a hair, pick the nearest choice — the difference is rounding, not an error.

**Two habits that save points**

1. **Answers stack.** Press `enter`, and the previous answer is available as `Ans` — the calculator reuses it if you immediately press an operator. Chain calculations instead of writing down and retyping an intermediate value.
2. **Use `▲` to recall and edit the last entry** instead of retyping a long expression when you realise one number was wrong. Press `▲`, arrow to the digit, `delete`, retype, `enter`.

### Working backwards

Two techniques that convert hard algebra into easy arithmetic. Use them whenever the direct route is not instantly obvious. They cost 60–90 seconds and they are nearly error-proof, which is exactly the trade you want on a test where 30 right is a pass.

**Technique 1 — plug the answer choices in (for "solve for x" and "which value satisfies" items)**

Works whenever the choices are plain numbers and the question asks for a value.

1. Write the equation or the condition from the stem.
2. **Start with choice B or C**, not A. The choices are almost always in numerical order, so a middle start tells you which direction to move if it fails.
3. Substitute the number everywhere the variable appears. Compute both sides separately.
4. If the two sides match, that is the answer — stop, do not test the rest.
5. If the left side came out too big, the answer is a smaller choice; too small, a larger choice. One more test usually finishes it.

Worked example. **If 5x − 7 = 3x + 11, what is x?** with choices 4, 7, 9, 12.

1. Try C, x = 9. Left: 5(9) − 7 = 45 − 7 = **38**. Right: 3(9) + 11 = 27 + 11 = **38**. Match. Answer is 9. Done in two lines with no algebra at all.

This also works on word problems: **A rectangle's length is 4 m more than its width and the perimeter is 36 m. What is the width?** Try each width choice, add 4 for the length, compute 2l + 2w, see which gives 36. Width 7 → length 11 → 2(11) + 2(7) = 22 + 14 = 36. Answer 7.

**When you cannot work backwards:** numeric-entry items (no choices), "which expression represents…" items where the choices are expressions (use Technique 2 instead), and choices that are not in order.

**Technique 2 — pick numbers (for variable-heavy and "which expression" items)**

Works whenever the stem and every answer choice contain variables and no specific numbers.

1. Pick a small, ugly-proof number for each variable. Good defaults: **x = 2, 3, or 5**. For percent questions pick **100**. For "a number of items" pick **10**.
2. **Avoid 0 and 1** — they make too many wrong choices accidentally work. Avoid a number that already appears in the problem.
3. Run the **story** with your number and get a target value.
4. Plug the same number into every answer choice. The one that produces your target is the answer.
5. If two choices tie, pick a second number and retest only those two.

Worked example. **Maya buys n notebooks at 3 dollars each and pays 5 dollars shipping. Which expression gives her total cost in dollars?** Choices: 3 + 5n, 3n + 5, 8n, 5n − 3.

1. Pick n = 4.
2. Story: 4 notebooks × 3 = 12 dollars, plus 5 dollars shipping = **17 dollars**.
3. Test: 3 + 5(4) = 23 ✗. 3(4) + 5 = **17** ✓. 8(4) = 32 ✗. 5(4) − 3 = 17 ✓ — a tie, so test n = 2: story gives 2(3) + 5 = 11; 3(2) + 5 = **11** ✓; 5(2) − 3 = 7 ✗.
4. Answer: **3n + 5**.

Worked example for percent. **A price is increased by 20% and then reduced by 20%. The final price is what percent of the original?**

1. Pick original = **100**.
2. Up 20%: 100 × 1.20 = **120**. Down 20%: 120 × 0.80 = **96**.
3. 96 out of 100 = **96%**. (The trap choice is 100%.)

## How to find the answer

This is the mechanical routine. Run it on every item. It is deliberately boring.

**Step 1 — Read the last sentence first.** Find the actual question: "What is the area…", "How many…", "Which expression…", "What is the value of x…". Underline the noun you are being asked for and the **units** it is in. Half of all GED math errors are answering a different question from the one asked — solving for x when they wanted 2x, finding the radius when they wanted the diameter, finding the total when they wanted the difference.

**Step 2 — Read the stimulus and strip it to numbers with labels.** On your note board, write each given quantity as `value + unit + what it is`:

- `length = 12 ft`
- `width = 8 ft`
- `cost = 3.50 dollars per sq ft`
- `WANT: total cost, in dollars`

Do not start computing while reading. Extract first, compute second. For a table or graph, do not read every cell — find the row and column headings that match the words in the question, and read only that intersection.

**Step 3 — Name the operation before you touch the calculator.** Say, in words, what the answer is: "total cost is area times price per square foot." Nearly every GED item reduces to one of about ten sentences:

- a part of a whole → multiply by a decimal (percent)
- a whole from a part → divide by a decimal (reverse percent)
- a per-unit rate → divide
- two quantities compared as "how much more" → subtract
- "of" → multiply; "per" and "for each" → divide; "total", "combined", "altogether" → add; "left", "remaining", "difference" → subtract
- a shape with a formula → go to the formula sheet
- "how many can I buy / how many fit" → divide, then round **down**
- "how many trips / buses / boxes do I need" → divide, then round **up**
- an unknown in a relationship → write an equation, then solve
- "is this always true" → test with numbers

**Step 4 — Go to the Formula Sheet if a shape, a line, a quadratic, or interest is involved.** Click the button. Copy the formula onto your board **with the given numbers already substituted** before you evaluate it. Writing `V = πr²h → V = π(3)²(10)` on the board and then calculating is dramatically more reliable than doing it all in the calculator.

**Step 5 — Compute, in one chain, on the calculator (Part 2) or on paper (Part 1).** Use `Ans` to chain. Do not round intermediate values — round only at the end, and only to the precision the question asked for.

**Step 6 — Check the answer against the question and against sense.**

- **Units check.** Did they want feet or square feet? Dollars or cents? Hours or minutes?
- **Size check.** A discount makes a price smaller. A 6% increase on 200 is about 212, not 1,200. The hypotenuse is the longest side. A probability is between 0 and 1. An area in a 10×10 grid is not 400.
- **Question check.** Re-read the underlined question. Did you find **that**?
- **Reasonableness of the choice you picked.** If three choices cluster (12.5, 12.8, 13.1) and one is far off (125), the far one is usually the decimal-slip trap.

**Step 7 — Eliminate rather than solve, when solving is slow.**

- Cross out any choice with the **wrong sign**, the wrong **units**, or an impossible **magnitude**.
- On "which equation models this," test the choices at **one easy input** from the story — usually the starting value, where x = 0. The correct equation must reproduce the story's starting number.
- On graph/line questions, check **two things** only: the y-intercept (where it crosses the vertical axis) and whether the slope is positive or negative. Two checks usually kill three choices.
- On geometry, **estimate**: sketch, judge whether the answer should be roughly 50 or roughly 500, and eliminate.
- On factoring questions, **multiply the choices back out** — expanding is faster and safer than factoring.

**Step 8 — For numeric entry, do the arithmetic twice.** There is no choice to sanity-check you against and there is no partial credit. Recompute by a slightly different route (e.g. compute 15% as ×0.15 the first time and as 10% + 5% the second). Then check the formatting: rounding as instructed, no units typed, no commas, minus sign from the keyboard.

**Step 9 — For drag-and-drop and hot spot, place, then re-read the prompt.** These items are easy to answer correctly and record wrongly. Confirm every slot is filled and the clicked point is on the intersection you meant, not one square off.

## The traps

**1. Answering the sub-step instead of the question.** You solve 3x + 6 = 21, get x = 5, and choose 5 — but the question asked for the perimeter, which is 4x = 20. **Fix:** underline the question's noun in Step 1 and re-read it before you click. The wrong-but-tempting choice is nearly always a correct intermediate value.

**2. Percent change measured against the wrong number.** Price goes 80 → 100. People compute 20/100 = 20%. It is 20/80 = **25%**. **Fix:** the denominator is always the **original / starting / earlier / "was"** value. Write "over the OLD one" on your board once at the start of the test.

**3. Adding successive percents.** A 20% raise then a 10% raise is not 30%. It is × 1.20 × 1.10 = × 1.32 = **32%**. A 25% markup then a 25% markdown is not break-even, it is × 1.25 × 0.75 = **93.75%** of the original. **Fix:** successive percents **multiply**.

**4. Reverse percent solved by multiplying.** "After a 20% discount the jacket cost 64 dollars. What was the original price?" People compute 64 × 1.20 = 76.80. Correct: 64 is 80% of the original, so 64 ÷ 0.80 = **80 dollars**. **Fix:** if the given number is the **result** of a percent, **divide**.

**5. The `(-)` versus `−` mix-up, and the (−4)² error.** `(-)` `4` `x²` gives −16; `(` `(-)` `4` `)` `x²` gives 16. **Fix:** parenthesise every negative you square. And when a formula has a subtraction of a negative, write it on the board as 7 − (−3) = 7 + 3 = 10 before typing.

**6. Order of operations run left to right.** 8 + 2 × 5 is 18, not 50. 20 − 6 ÷ 2 is 17, not 7. Multiplication and division bind before addition and subtraction; within each tier you go left to right, so 24 ÷ 4 × 2 = **12**, not 3. **Fix:** on Part 1 (no calculator), circle the ×, ÷ and exponents first and do them before anything else.

**7. Perimeter/area/volume confusion and unit slips.** Perimeter is a length (ft), area is squared (ft²), volume is cubed (ft³). Converting 2 yd to feet is ×3 = 6 ft, but 2 yd² to ft² is **×9**, and 2 yd³ to ft³ is **×27**. **Fix:** check the exponent on the unit in the answer choices — it tells you which formula they wanted.

**8. Using diameter where the formula wants radius.** "A circular table is 6 feet across." That is the diameter; r = 3. A = π(3)² = 28.3 ft², not π(6)² = 113. **Fix:** the words **across, wide, diameter** all mean you must halve before using A = πr², C = 2πr, or V = πr²h.

**9. Mislabeling the hypotenuse.** a² + b² = c² only when **c is the side opposite the right angle**. If the problem gives the hypotenuse and one leg and asks for the other leg, you **subtract**: b = √(c² − a²). A 13-ft ladder 5 ft from the wall reaches √(169 − 25) = √144 = **12 ft**, not √(169 + 25). **Fix:** identify the right angle first; the side that does not touch it is c.

**10. Forgetting the second solution of a quadratic.** x² = 49 has solutions **7 and −7**. x² − 5x − 14 = 0 gives x = 7 and x = −2. **Fix:** factoring always produces two roots; the quadratic formula's ± always produces two. If the question is a real-world one (a length, a number of people, a time), **discard the negative root** — but only then.

**11. Distributing a negative to only the first term.** −3(x − 4) = −3x **+ 12**, not −3x − 12. And 5 − (2x + 3) = 5 − 2x − 3 = **2 − 2x**. **Fix:** draw arrows from the multiplier to every term inside before simplifying.

**12. Flipping (or not flipping) the inequality sign.** Dividing or multiplying an inequality by a **negative** reverses it: −2x > 8 gives x **<** −4. Adding or subtracting never reverses it. **Fix:** the only trigger is multiplying/dividing by a negative — nothing else.

**13. Reading slope upside down.** Slope is **rise over run**, (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). People compute (x₂ − x₁)/(y₂ − y₁), or subtract the coordinates in a different order on top than on bottom. **Fix:** label the points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) on the board, and keep the **same point first** in both the numerator and the denominator.

**14. Mean/median confusion and the "average of averages" error.** Median needs the list **sorted first** — the #1 median mistake is taking the middle of an unsorted list. And the average of two averages is only valid if the group sizes are equal: 20 students averaging 80 and 30 averaging 90 gives (20×80 + 30×90)/50 = (1600 + 2700)/50 = **86**, not 85. **Fix:** sort before you take a median; use total ÷ total count for combined averages.

**15. Probability "without replacement."** Two red from a bag of 5 red and 5 blue: (5/10) × (4/9) = 20/90 = **2/9**, not (5/10)² = 1/4. **Fix:** if the first item is not returned, both the numerator and the denominator drop by 1 for the second draw.

**16. Rounding too early.** Rounding π to 3.14 at step one, then multiplying by a large number, shifts the answer past the nearest choice. **Fix:** keep everything on the calculator; round once, at the end, to the stated precision.

**17. Numeric-entry formatting.** Typing "37.50 with a dollar sign" or "37.5 ft" or "1,250" in a box that wants a bare number can score zero. **Fix:** digits, one decimal point, and a minus sign only. Match the rounding instruction exactly — "to the nearest tenth" means 37.5, not 37.50 and not 37.

**18. Burning Part 1 time.** The five no-calculator items are worth the same as any other five and they are placed first, when you are freshest and most anxious. People spend 25 minutes there. **Fix:** hard cap Part 1 at **12 minutes**. If an item is not yielding in 2½ minutes, answer something plausible and submit — you cannot return, so a guess now costs nothing later.

## Pacing

**The arithmetic of the clock:** 115 minutes ÷ 46 questions = **2 minutes 30 seconds per question**. That is generous for this test. Most items are 60–90 seconds; a handful are 4 minutes. The budget works if you refuse to let any single item eat the surplus.

**Split the plan by part:**

| Segment | Questions | Target time | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part 1 (no calculator) | 5 | 12 min | 12 min |
| Break | — | 3 min (free) | 12 min |
| Part 2, first pass on Q6–Q25 | 20 | 42 min | 54 min |
| Part 2, first pass on Q26–Q46 | 21 | 46 min | 100 min |
| Review flagged + verify entries | — | 15 min | 115 min |

**Checkpoints to glance at, and nothing else:**

- After Part 1: **12 minutes gone**. If you are at 20, speed up immediately — stop re-checking work.
- **Halfway in questions (Q23) should be near 55 minutes.** If you are past 65, start using the 90-second rule below aggressively.
- **Q40 by 95 minutes.** If you are behind here, go straight to triage: answer every remaining item with your best 30-second effort rather than solving 3 of them perfectly and leaving 3 blank.

**The 90-second rule.** Give every question 90 seconds of genuine attempt. At 90 seconds, make a decision:

1. **Can I eliminate at least two choices?** If yes, spend 30 more seconds and choose between the survivors. Then move on regardless.
2. **Can I not even start?** Choose a letter, **flag it**, move on. Do not read it again now.
3. **Am I mid-calculation and close?** Give it 45 more seconds, then stop and answer with your best value. Numbers that take four minutes are not worth two that take one each.

**Never leave anything blank.** No wrong-answer penalty. Blanks and wrong answers score identically, so a blank is a pure donation. For multiple choice, guessing gives you 25%. For numeric entry, put down something the size the problem implies — for an area question about a 12-by-8 room, typing 96 costs nothing and occasionally hits.

**Which to skip on sight, on a first pass:** long multi-step geometry with an unusual solid; a system-of-equations word problem with three sentences of setup; any item where you do not recognise the vocabulary in the first 10 seconds. Flag them. They are worth the same 1 point as a percent question you can do in 40 seconds.

**The last 10 minutes.** In priority order:

1. **Fill every blank.** Open the question navigator and look for unanswered items. This is the highest-value 90 seconds on the test. Answer them all, even randomly.
2. **Re-check every numeric-entry answer's format** — rounding, no units, no commas, correct sign. These are the answers most likely to be right in your head and wrong in the box.
3. **Revisit flagged items where you had eliminated two choices.** You are picking between two, so a fresh 60 seconds is worth a lot. Skip flagged items you never started — there is no time to learn them now.
4. **Re-read the question line (not your work) on any item where you remember doing a multi-step calculation.** You are hunting for "answered the wrong question," which is the most common recoverable error.
5. **Do not re-do arithmetic you already checked.** Changing a correct answer to a wrong one on a hunch is a real and common way to lose the two points you needed.

Submit when the clock is gone, not before. There is no bonus for finishing early and no risk in using every second.

## If you only have three days

Ranked by points-per-hour-of-study. Do them strictly in this order and stop where your time stops. Items 1–5 alone reach roughly 60% of the test's content.

**1. Percent, in all four directions (highest yield on the whole test).** Percent of a number; percent off; percent increase and decrease; **percent change with the original on the bottom**; finding the original from a discounted price; successive percents multiplying; simple interest I = prt (it is on the sheet, so just practise plugging in). Drill until "64 dollars after a 20% discount" makes you write `64 ÷ 0.80` without thinking. **Budget: 90 minutes.**

**2. Solving linear equations and translating word problems into them.** Two-step, multi-step, variables on both sides, distributing first, equations with fractions (multiply everything by the denominator). Then the translation patterns: "5 more than twice a number" = 2n + 5; "a number decreased by 7" = n − 7; "the total cost is a flat fee plus a per-item charge" = b + mx. Pair this with **working backwards** from the answer choices — it makes half these items trivial. **Budget: 2 hours.**

**3. Slope, y-intercept, and the equation of a line.** Slope from two points (formula is on the sheet), slope from a graph (rise/run), slope from a table (change in y ÷ change in x), and above all **what slope and intercept mean in a story** ("the slope means the cost rises 4 dollars per hour; the intercept is the 25 dollar base fee"). Then y = mx + b given a slope and a point, and given two points. This is about 14% of the test and it is pure pattern. **Budget: 2 hours.**

**4. Function notation and reading functions from tables and graphs.** f(x) means "put this in, get that out": f(3) means substitute 3. Solving f(x) = 20 means setting the expression equal to 20. Reading a value off a table or graph. Comparing a function given as a table against one given as an equation (evaluate both at the same input). **Budget: 60 minutes.**

**5. The formula sheet drill — geometry by lookup, not by memory.** Do not memorise the shapes. Spend the time learning to **find** the right entry fast and substitute correctly: rectangle and triangle area, circle area and circumference (halve the diameter!), volume of a rectangular prism and a cylinder, surface area of a rectangular prism, and the Pythagorean theorem including the subtract-to-find-a-leg case. Also practise **composite figures**: split an L-shape into two rectangles, add; find a shaded region by subtracting. **Budget: 2 hours.**

**6. Fractions and decimals without a calculator, for Part 1.** Common denominators; multiply straight across; divide by flipping the second fraction; converting between mixed and improper; the fraction/decimal/percent table above; and order of operations by hand. Part 1 is only 5 questions but it is the part where a calculator cannot save you. **Budget: 60 minutes.**

**7. The calculator, hands-on.** Twenty minutes, once: `(-)` vs `−`, the fraction template and the `►` exit, the `◄►` toggle, `x²`, `^`, `2nd x²` for √, `x10ⁿ`. Do it on the official free practice test so the on-screen version is what you have touched. **Budget: 30 minutes.**

**8. Mean, median, range, and reading graphs and tables.** Sort before taking a median. Find the missing value needed to hit a target mean (target × count − current total). Read a bar/line graph for a specific labelled value and for a difference between two bars. **Budget: 45 minutes.**

**9. Inequalities and systems.** Solve a one-variable inequality, remembering the sign flips only on multiplying/dividing by a negative; interpret "at most" (≤), "at least" (≥), "no more than" (≤), "more than" (>). Systems: substitution when one equation is already solved for a variable, elimination when the coefficients line up — and remember you can just test the answer choices in both equations. **Budget: 60 minutes.**

**10. Quadratics, last.** Factoring x² + bx + c (find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b), difference of squares, FOIL to expand, and using the **quadratic formula off the sheet** when factoring does not work. Roughly 3% of the test. It is last for a reason — if you run out of time, skip it and accept the 1–2 items. **Budget: 45 minutes, only if items 1–9 are solid.**

**What to cut entirely if you are short:** surface area of cones and pyramids, permutations and combinations beyond simple multiplication, geometric sequences, domain and range as formal vocabulary, and anything involving three-variable systems (not tested). Together these are worth a handful of points, and you do not need them to clear 145.

**The night before:** do one timed 46-question practice set, score it, and then spend the remaining time **only** on the categories you missed in items 1–5 above. Do not learn a new topic in the last 12 hours — convert near-misses into hits instead.
